Collider Bias in 3D

Dr. R. Düsing · Osnabrück University
If you “control” for a common effect, you create an association between its causes that does not exist. Here you see it in a 3D cube.
What is this about?
Talent and effort have nothing to do with each other among people who apply. Later job success, however, depends on both. If in the analysis you “partial out” job success (or look only at the most successful), it suddenly looks as if talented people were less hard-working: whoever has more talent at the same level of success needs less effort, and vice versa. Job success is a collider here: both variables act on it (talent → success ← effort). This tool shows that in 3D. Every point is a person with talent, effort and success; colour shows success. Seen from above the cloud is round, there is no association. If you partial out success, the cloud is flattened and the falling diagonal that was already hidden in the colour bands becomes visible. Berkson's Paradox shows the same trap through selecting cases; here it is about controlling in an analysis.
How to proceed
1. Causal structure (DAG) — understand talent → success ← effort as a collider.
2. Basic principle — the formulas and the slope of effort on talent without and with success as a control variable.
3. The 3D cube — switch conditioning (off, partial out, select) and change the viewing direction: from above (X–Y), from the front (X–Z), from the side (Y–Z).
4. The three side views — show live what the analysis “sees”, with a regression line.
5. Concepts — what partialling out means geometrically, and when you can and cannot recognise a collider in data.
What to watch for
Selecting, controlling and partialling out are three routes to the same error: you compare only people with (almost) the same value on the collider.
The numbers alone do not reveal whether controlling closed a path or opened one. With a confounder a spurious association disappears, with a collider one appears. Both change the estimate. Your causal model decides, not the data.
If X truly affects Y, controlling for the collider does not create a correlation out of nothing. Instead the estimate becomes biased: it shrinks or even changes sign.
Things to try
Choose scenario B (“partial out Z”): the cloud tips from round to a falling diagonal, and the correlation r(X,Y | Z) becomes clearly negative. Then choose scenario D: talent has a true positive effect on effort (β = +0.40), but after controlling for Z the estimated slope even turns negative.

Help — Collider bias in 3D

What does this tool show?

Two variables X (talent) and Y (effort) that have nothing to do with each other in the population are simulated together with a common effect Z (job success). The tool shows as a 3D cloud what happens when you condition on Z in the analysis: by partialling out (Z as a control variable) or by selecting cases with high Z.

The running example

An HR department rates applications by talent (X, IQ test) and effort (Y, self-report). Job success Z depends on both: Z = X + Y + noise. With the β slider, talent can also have a true effect on effort (Y = β·X + e). Then the question is how well β is still estimated.

The mechanism (why?)

The points lie on a tilted surface Z ≈ X + Y. Within equal Z values (same colour), X + Y ≈ constant: more talent goes with less effort. Without conditioning all Z layers are mixed and the diagonals cancel in the sum (r ≈ 0). When conditioning on Z only the diagonal within a layer remains. Z is a collider: it has two incoming arrows (X → Z ← Y).

What does “holding Z constant” mean?

Partialling out answers the question: How are X and Y related if all people had the same success Z? In the cube it looks as if all points were pressed together onto the plane Z = 0. That is only the picture for “Z is now the same for everybody”. Important: the points are not pressed straight down but shifted along the slope of the surface. A point with high Z slides to smaller values of X and Y (high success already explains part of talent and effort), a point with low Z to larger values. Pressing straight down alone would not change the view from above at all. Only this shift in X and Y removes the part that Z explains. What remains is what is in X and Y in addition to Z (the residuals). There, anyone with more talent at the same success has less effort.

Partialling out or selecting?

Partialling out: from X and Y the part that Z predicts linearly is subtracted (residuals). The correlation of the residuals is the partial correlation r(X,Y | Z); the slope of the residuals is the coefficient of X in Y ~ X + Z. In the cube the tilted surface is flattened.
Selecting: only cases with high Z enter the sample (clinic, scholarship, follow-up). The yellow plane is the threshold. Both routes look at people with similar Z and therefore give a negative correlation. The numbers differ because selection uses only part of the data.

The 3D cube

Drag with the mouse to rotate. The buttons below the cube jump to fixed viewing directions: from above shows X against Y (the view of the analysis), from the front X against Z and from the side Y against Z. Colour shows the value of Z (blue low, orange high).

The key figures

r(X,Y) · all points: correlation without conditioning. r(X,Y | Z): correlation of the residuals. r(X,Y) · selection: correlation within the selection only. b without → with Z: regression slope of Y on X without and with Z as a control variable.

Controls

Scenarios: four presets. Conditioning: off, partial out, or select high Z. Selection: share of cases with the highest Z. β: true effect of X on Y (default 0). Noise in Z: how much of Z does not depend on X and Y; more noise weakens the effect. n: sample size.

The arrow view

The section “Partialling out as arrows” shows the same calculation as a flat X–Y view: raw data on the left, in the middle an arrow for each point from the original value to the residual, the result on the right. The “Partialling out” slider (or ▶ Play) runs from 0% to 100%. The colour shows why the cloud tilts: high Z (orange) is pushed down-left, low Z (blue) up-right. Hover over a point to see it in all three panels.

Partialling out as arrows
Each arrow shows how a single point is shifted when Z is partialled out: from the original value (X, Y) to the residual (X*, Y*). Colour shows Z. Why the shift? Z contains part of X and of Y (Z = X + Y + noise). Someone with a high Z therefore tends to have higher X and Y as well, not because talent and effort are related but because both push Z up. To ask what holds at the same Z, you subtract this part predicted by Z: the higher Z, the further the point is pushed back (down-left); the lower Z, the further it is pushed forward (up-right). This turns the cloud into the falling diagonal. Use the slider to run the shift step by step.

Δ (delta) = shift. ΔX and ΔY are the amount by which the point is shifted when Z is removed. This is the part of X and Y that Z “co-explains”: bxz·Z and byz·Z. The minus sign means this part is subtracted. At high Z, ΔX is negative (the point moves left), at low Z positive (right). This is the length and direction of the arrow in the middle panel.

X* and Y* (read “X star”) = residuals. These are the values after the shift: X* = X + ΔX, Y* = Y + ΔY. They tell how much talent or effort a person has above or below what their success Z would lead you to expect. The arrowhead in the middle panel and the point in the right panel sit at (X*, Y*). The correlation r(X*, Y*) is the partial correlation r(X,Y | Z).

Concepts
Partialling out = correlating the residuals
A partial correlation r(X,Y | Z) is obtained by removing from X and from Y the part linearly explained by Z (the residuals) and then correlating the residuals. This corresponds to the regression Y ~ X + Z: the coefficient of X is the slope of the residuals. In the cube this means: the tilted surface is flattened along the Z axis, and the points slide to a common height (not straight down but along the slope of the surface, see Help). What remains in the X–Y view is exactly what holds within equal Z values: a falling diagonal.
Selecting and partialling out: two routes, one mechanism
When selecting (Berkson's Paradox) you look only at cases with high Z; when partialling out you compare within each Z layer and average. In both cases you see X and Y at (roughly) the same Z, and there X + Y ≈ constant. The difference lies in the numbers: selection uses only part of the data and therefore usually gives a weaker association than the residual correlation over all data. The equality “residual correlation = correlation within a layer” holds exactly only for linear-Gaussian data such as simulated here.
Collider or confounder? The same calculation, the opposite consequence
If you control for a variable Z and the relationship between X and Y changes, you do not yet know why. With a confounder (common cause) you removed a spurious association; with a collider (common effect) you created one. In the numbers both cases look similar. That is why “control for it if the estimate changes” is not a good rule. Which role Z plays is stated in the causal model. Causal Terror ↗ in the Bayes Thinking Lab practises exactly this.
What if there is a true effect?
If X really has an effect β on Y, the association is already there without conditioning. Controlling for the collider then does not create a new association but biases the estimate of β: it gets smaller or changes sign. Choose scenario D (β = +0.40, partial out Z) and compare the slope without and with Z. The same bias lurks in every study in which a collider (for example participation in a follow-up) is controlled for or becomes a condition for the sample.
Can you see a collider in the data?
Only under favourable conditions. In the isolated triangle of this tool X and Y are independent and become dependent only after conditioning on Z. That is a hint about the structure. As soon as X and Y are related anyway (for example through the true effect β), this hint disappears. From data alone you then cannot tell whether a variable is a collider. In practice this knowledge almost always comes from the study design: who is observed at all, who drops out, who is selected?
Consequences for research
Collider bias is common and hard to spot. Examples from psychology: dropout in therapy studies (therapy and symptom severity both influence who attends the follow-up; analysing only those who attend biases the effect), clinical samples (two disorders that both lead to treatment), admission procedures (grades and interview both determine admission; among the admitted they correlate negatively) and published studies (effect size and sample size both determine whether a study appears). Wherever participation depends on X and Y, Berkson lurks.
Distinction from confounding
Confounding: a common cause U → X and U → Y creates a spurious correlation, and controlling for U removes it. Collider bias: a common effect X → Z ← Y creates the spurious correlation only once you control for Z or select on it. Both bias, but with opposite logic. “Control for all available variables” is therefore not a safe strategy.
Descendants of a collider
A consequence of Z (a variable D with Z → D) also carries information about Z. If you control for D instead of Z, you open the path between X and Y at least partially, like a half adjustment for Z. Example: not job success itself but the later salary that depends on it enters the analysis. The tool does not show this case, but the logic is the same.
Recognising and avoiding it
Steps: (1) Draw the DAG before the analysis begins. (2) Classify all variables as confounders, mediators or colliders. (3) Adjust only for confounders, never for colliders. (4) Check who ends up in the sample: does participation depend on X and Y at the same time? (5) Run sensitivity analyses with other assumptions. Literature: Berkson (1946); Pearl (2009) Causality; Hernán & Robins (2020) Causal Inference.
